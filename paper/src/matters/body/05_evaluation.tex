\section{Results and Evaluation}
\label{sec:results-and-evaluation}

The following section presents the results of the multi-tier simulation across the 336-hour flood horizon.
We evaluate the impact of infrastructure coupling on the integrated robustness of accessibility ($RoA_{\text{Int}}$) and verify the research hypotheses regarding functional gaps and recovery lag.

\subsection{Scope and Methodological Baseline}
\label{subsec:scope-and-baseline}

Our Level A (Road) baseline yields $RoA_{\text{Int}} = 84.58\,\%$ and a systemic nadir ($RoA_{\text{Sys,min}}$) of $38.41\,\%$ during HQ100.
This differs from the $70.1\,\%$ benchmark by Kaub et al.~\cite{kaub2024}, reached during HQ100, due to our narrower municipal scope ($\sim 117\,\text{km}^2$, $12$ stations, $5,817$ nodes vs.~their regional $44$ stations, $26,853$ nodes).
The resulting lack of suburban redundancy, paired with localized Trier gauge hydrodynamic data, yields a conservative, high-fidelity assessment.
Consequently, we evaluate performance by comparing Level A (baseline $RoA$) directly against Level C (compound effects) rather than Kaub et al.'s regional benchmark.

\subsubsection{H1: Functional Gap Analysis}
\label{subsubsec:h1-analysis}
Hypothesis H1 is confirmed by a $1.12\,\%$ peak nadir ($RoA_{\text{Sys,min}}$) gap between Levels A ($38.41\,\%$) and C ($37.29\,\%$). The $6.38\,\%$ integrated resilience ($RoA_{\text{Int}}$) gap reflects the 336-hour window, with disruption concentrated in peak and recovery phases due to fire station utility exhaustion.

\subsubsection{H2: Temporal Hysteresis and Recovery Lag}
\label{subsubsec:h2-analysis}
Hypothesis H2 is confirmed: Level A restores at hour 288 (recession end), while Level C remains compromised until hour 336. This 48-hour lag reflects FSM reboot delays and hysteresis thresholds during post-inundation decontamination and restarts.

\subsection{Spatial Breakdown and District-Level Vulnerability}
\label{subsec:spatial-breakdown-and-district-level-vulnerability}

The spatial distribution of the functional gap $\Delta RoA_j$ at peak flood (HQ100) is visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:spatial-gap}.
The primary driver of this disparity is the district of Ruwer-Eitelsbach.
Because surrounding roads are entirely inundated, hospital accessibility drops identically to $0.00\,\%$ in both Level A and Level C\@.
However, coverage metrics diverge significantly regarding fire services.
In Level A, the local fire station is modeled as operational because its physical facility remains above the water line.
In Level C, off-site power station failures and exhausted facility power buffer renders the station non-functional despite remaining dry.
This cascading utility failure leaves the isolated district completely without emergency services, explaining why fire stations, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:multi-tier-ts}, exhibit a substantially larger functional gap than hospitals.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{resources/combined_resilience_map_ruwer}
    \caption{Heatmap of the functional gap $\Delta RoA_j$ across Trier districts. High-intensity areas indicate where utility failures compounded transport disruptions.}
    \label{fig:spatial-gap}
\end{figure}

\noindent
As visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:spatial-gap}, Ruwer-Eitelsbach experiences the highest overestimation gap (mean $31.99\,\%$, reaching up to $50.00\,\%$) from compounded road inundation and facility buffer exhaustion. Spatially, hospital centralization causes uniform accessibility losses in peripheral districts, whereas the distributed fire station network maintains higher baseline resilience despite localized utility outages dropping its nadir from $76.82\,\%$ to $74.59\,\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:multi-tier-ts}).

\subsection{Conclusion of Evaluation}
\label{subsec:evaluation-conclusion}

The results demonstrate that evaluating critical infrastructure through a single-infrastructure lens significantly underestimates the systemic risk.
The confirmation of H1 and H2 validates the necessity of compound multi-infrastructure modeling for urban disaster resilience planning.
In particular, the 48-hour recovery lag identified in Level C highlights a critical policy window for emergency logistics during the post-flood phase.
