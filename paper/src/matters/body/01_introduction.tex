\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern societies depend heavily on the continuous functioning of critical infrastructure (CI), including water, healthcare, energy supplies, and transportation networks~\cite{lickert2024,chiaradonna2007}.
These systems are characterized by complex functional interdependencies, meaning that disruptions in one sector can propagate to others, potentially causing widespread cascading failures~\cite{laprie2007,buldyrev2010}.
When evaluating crisis scenarios, it is therefore insufficient to consider infrastructures in isolation; instead, one must understand how failures spread over time and across sectoral boundaries~\cite{lickert2024}.

A critical aspect of infrastructure resilience is the ability to provide essential services to the population.
This depends not only on the operational status of a \textbf{Point of Interest} (POI), such as a hospital, but also on its accessibility via the road network~\cite{kaub2024}.
Kaub et al.\ recently introduced the \textbf{Robustness of Accessibility} (RoA) approach to quantify how road network disruptions affect the accessibility of individual POIs~\cite{kaub2024}.
However, existing research often treats traffic-related accessibility and the temporal propagation of failures in utility grids separately.
While the RoA focuses on road-based resilience, other models, such as those by Lickert et al., focus on functional cascades within interdependent utility infrastructures~\cite{lickert2024}.

Despite these advancements, a significant research gap remains: it is currently unclear how cascading failures in utility systems and traffic-related accessibility jointly impact the overall resilience of a region over time.
This leads to the central research question of this work: \textit{How do cascade effects in utility infrastructures influence the time-dependent reachability (RoA) of critical POIs?}

\noindent \newline
To address this question, we examine the following hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{H1:} The inclusion of power and water outage cascades results in a lower resilience assessment than a purely road-based RoA analysis would suggest.
    \item \textbf{H2:} By modeling \textit{lifetimes}, the period during which a CI remains functional despite the loss of a supply service (e.g., via emergency power), it is possible to identify critical time windows for disaster management.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The primary goal of this paper is to methodologically link the road-based RoA assessment with time-dependent modeling of cascading failures.
We extend the RoA Index to incorporate the operational capability of POIs based on their electricity and water supply status.
Using the \textit{lifetime} concept~\cite{lickert2024}, we classify infrastructures as inoperable once bridging times have elapsed, providing a dynamic resilience analysis.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews related work and identifies the research gap.
Section 3 introduces our methodology, including the functional RoA extension and the Finite State Machine (FSM) model.
Section 4 details the technical implementation and the Trier case study.
Section 5 evaluates the results and tests our hypotheses.
Section 6 discusses findings and limitations, while Section 7 concludes the paper and offers an outlook on future research directions.
